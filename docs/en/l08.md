## From "what to buy" to "how to combine"

You now know about index funds. The next questions are: **How many? How much of each? When to buy?**

This lesson covers three things:

1. **Asset allocation**: how much in stocks versus bonds.
2. **Dollar-cost averaging**: investing a fixed amount on a schedule.
3. **Rebalancing**: periodically bringing the mix back on target.

Settle these three and you have a plan you can follow for years.

## Asset allocation: the most important decision

**Asset allocation** is how you split your money across asset types, mainly **stocks versus bonds**.

Why does it matter so much? Because how much a portfolio swings depends mostly on how much stock it holds:

| Mix (stocks/bonds) | Style | Character |
|---|---|---|
| 90 / 10 | Aggressive | High growth potential; can be cut in half in a crash |
| 60 / 40 | Balanced | The classic mix; much calmer than all stocks |
| 30 / 70 | Conservative | Smaller swings, slower long-run growth |

**Stocks** are the growth engine; **bonds** are the shock absorber. When stocks crash, high-quality bonds usually fall less and sometimes rise, cushioning the overall loss.

### How to choose

There's no single right answer, but think about three things:

1. **Time**: when will you need the money? The longer the horizon, the more stock volatility you can absorb (Lesson 2).
2. **Tolerance**: if your portfolio fell 30%, could you still sleep?
3. **Need**: what return do your goals actually require?

A popular rough guide: **bond percentage ≈ your age**, or the more aggressive **stock percentage ≈ 110 − your age**. At 30, that suggests roughly 70%–80% stocks. It's a starting point, not a rule.

## Two simple ways to build it

### Option 1: the three-fund portfolio

Three low-cost index funds cover the world's stocks and US bonds:

| Fund type | Role | Examples (illustration only) |
|---|---|---|
| US total stock market | Growth engine | VTI / FSKAX |
| International stocks | Global diversification | VXUS / FTIHX |
| US aggregate bonds | Shock absorber | BND / FXNAX |

A 30-year-old who can handle big swings might choose **50% US stocks + 20% international stocks + 30% bonds**, or something more aggressive. The numbers here are just an example.

### Option 2: a target-date fund (all in one)

A **target-date fund** has a year in its name, like "2060 Fund", meaning it's built for people retiring around 2060.

- It already holds a mix of stocks and bonds.
- As the target year approaches, it **automatically** lowers its stock share and gets more conservative. This path is called the **glide path**.
- Many 401(k) plans use these as the default investment.

The upside is simplicity: one fund is a complete portfolio. The downsides: fees are usually a bit higher than building it yourself from index funds, and different companies' funds for the same year can hold quite different mixes, so check before you buy.

## Dollar-cost averaging: a fixed amount, every month

**Dollar-cost averaging (DCA)** means investing a fixed amount on a regular schedule (say, monthly), whatever the market is doing.

Example, $300 a month:

| Month | Price | Shares bought |
|---|---|---|
| Jan | $50 | 6.0 |
| Feb | $40 | 7.5 |
| Mar | $25 | 12.0 |
| Apr | $40 | 7.5 |
| May | $50 | 6.0 |
| **Total** | Average price $41 | **39 shares for $1,500** |

- Your average cost is $1,500 ÷ 39 ≈ **$38.46**, below the average price of $41, because the same dollars bought more shares when the price was low.
- When the price returns to $50, your 39 shares are worth **$1,950**, a 30% gain, even though the price only came back to where it started.

The main benefit of DCA is that it's **easy to stick with**:

- No need to guess whether "now is a good time".
- In a downturn you're buying at a discount as planned, not panicking.
- It lines up with your paycheck and runs on autopilot.

### A big sum: all at once or spread out?

If you suddenly have a large amount (a bonus, an inheritance), a 2023 Vanguard study (global stock market data, 1976–2022) found that, looking one year out, **investing it all at once** beat spreading it over three months about **68%** of the time. The reason: markets rise more often than they fall, so money invested sooner usually grows sooner.

But if you worry that a drop right after investing would make you give up, spreading it over a few months is fine. **Choose the method you'll stick with.**

## Rebalancing: bring the mix back on target

Say your target is **60% stocks, 40% bonds**, and you invest $10,000. A year later stocks are up 30% and bonds up 2%:

| | Start | One year later | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stocks | $6,000 | $7,800 | 65.7% |
| Bonds | $4,000 | $4,080 | 34.3% |
| Total | $10,000 | $11,880 | |

Stocks drifted from 60% to about 66%, so the portfolio is riskier than you planned. **Rebalancing** brings it back to 60/40: sell about $672 of stocks and buy bonds, ending at $7,128 / $4,752.

Rebalancing makes you **sell what rose the most and buy what lagged**, the opposite of the human instinct to chase winners and dump losers.

### How to rebalance

- **By calendar**: check once a year on a fixed date (your birthday works).
- **By threshold**: adjust when an asset drifts more than 5 percentage points from target.
- **With new money**: instead of selling, direct new contributions to whatever is underweight. Easiest, and it avoids taxes on sales.

::: tip Mind the taxes
Selling at a profit in a taxable brokerage account triggers tax. When rebalancing, prefer to do it inside retirement accounts like a 401(k) or IRA, or use new money.
:::

## Putting it into a plan

You can now write a complete, simple plan. For example:

> I'm 28 and investing for retirement.
> Allocation: 80% stocks (55% US, 25% international) + 20% bonds.
> After each paycheck, $500 goes in automatically, Roth IRA first, using three index funds.
> Every January I check once; if anything is off by more than 5%, I rebalance with new money.
> In a crash I don't sell; I keep investing as planned.

Write the plan down. When markets crash, following a written plan is more reliable than deciding on the spot. Lesson 23 walks you through a fuller investment plan.

## Summary

- **Asset allocation** (the stock/bond mix) is the most important decision; it sets how much the portfolio swings.
- A **three-fund portfolio** or a **target-date fund** are both simple, effective options.
- **DCA** removes market timing and buys more when prices fall; a lump sum usually wins slightly, but sticking with it matters more than optimizing.
- **Rebalancing** pulls the mix back on target and makes you "buy low, sell high".

That's the end of the funds chapter. Index funds, asset allocation, DCA and rebalancing cover most people's long-term investing needs. The next chapters cover stocks and bonds, then more complex tools like options and futures.
