## Rates up, bond prices down

This is the most important rule in bonds: **when market interest rates rise, prices of existing fixed-rate bonds fall; when rates fall, prices rise.**

The reason is simple. Say you hold a 10-year bond with a $1,000 face value and a 3% coupon, paying $30 a year. A year later, market rates rise to 4%, and similar new bonds pay $40 a year.

If you want to sell now, nobody will pay $1,000 for an IOU that pays $30. The price has to drop until the buyer's yield to maturity is also close to 4%.

| | Rates unchanged (3%) | Rates rise to 4% |
|---|---|---|
| Annual interest | $30 | $30 (unchanged) |
| Market price with 9 years left | About $1,000 | About $925 |

This example comes from the SEC's investor site, investor.gov. If rates instead fell to 2%, your 3% bond would be in demand and trade above $1,000.

If you hold to maturity, price swings along the way don't change the $1,000 you get back. The price drop reflects an opportunity cost: you locked in 3% while the market now pays 4%.

## Duration: how sensitive a bond is

Bonds react to rates by different amounts. The longer the maturity and the lower the coupon, the more sensitive the price.

**Duration** turns that sensitivity into a number, measured in years. A handy approximation:

**Price change ≈ −duration × change in rates**

| Bond fund (example) | Average duration | Rates +1 point | Rates −1 point |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short-term Treasury fund | About 2 years | About −2% | About +2% |
| Total bond market fund | About 6 years | About −6% | About +6% |
| Long-term Treasury fund | About 16 years | About −16% | About +16% |

The durations above are illustrative; a real fund lists its duration on its fact sheet (Lesson 7).

::: tip Using duration
If you'll need the money within a few years, choose short-duration bonds or funds; rising rates hurt them less. For long-term holdings, the intermediate duration of a total bond market fund is a common choice. Reaching for yield with long-duration bonds means taking much more rate risk.
:::

## 2022: the worst year for bonds

At the end of 2021, US rates were very low. In 2022, inflation jumped, the Federal Reserve raised rates sharply and market rates followed:

- The Bloomberg US Aggregate Bond Index returned about **−13%** in 2022, the worst year in the index's history.
- The Bloomberg US Treasury Index returned **−12.5%**, also its biggest annual loss in more than four decades.

Stocks fell hard the same year, so many 60/40 portfolios took a large hit.

Two lessons from that year:

1. High-quality bonds can lose money; interest rate risk is real.
2. When starting yields are very low, interest is too small to offset falling prices. Once rates rose, bonds' interest income rose too.

## The yield curve

Plot Treasury yields against their maturities and you get the **yield curve**.

- **Normal**: longer maturities pay more. Lenders want more for tying up money longer.
- **Inverted**: short-term yields are above long-term yields.

An inversion is widely watched as a recession signal. A 2022 Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco study noted that the Treasury yield curve inverted before all 10 US recessions since 1955, with the recession usually following within a year or two. That study favors the spread between 10-year and 3-month Treasury yields.

::: warning A signal, not a schedule
An inversion means the market expects rates to fall, often because it expects the economy to slow. It can't tell you whether or when a recession comes, or what stocks will do. Don't time the market on the yield curve.
:::

## Three common Treasury products

| | T-Bills | TIPS | Series I Savings Bonds (I Bonds) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maturity | 4 to 52 weeks | 5, 10, 30 years | Up to 30 years, can be cashed early |
| Return | Bought at a discount, repaid at face value | Principal adjusts with CPI; interest is paid on the adjusted principal | Fixed rate + inflation rate, reset every six months |
| Where to buy | Broker or TreasuryDirect | Broker or TreasuryDirect | TreasuryDirect only |
| Limits | None | Selling early is at market price | Up to $10,000 a year in electronic bonds per person; hold at least 1 year; cashing in before 5 years costs the last 3 months of interest |
| State tax | Exempt | Exempt | Exempt |

- **T-Bills** are short, with very little rate risk, and suit money you'll need soon. Many money market funds hold mostly T-Bills.
- **TIPS** protect against inflation: the higher inflation runs, the more the principal grows. They still carry rate risk; TIPS funds also fell in 2022.
- **I Bonds** don't trade, so their value never drops, but they have purchase caps and holding rules. A TreasuryDirect account requires a US Social Security number and other details, so most non-US residents can't buy them.

## Summary

- When rates rise, existing bond prices fall; when rates fall, prices rise.
- **Duration** measures sensitivity: price change ≈ −duration × rate change.
- Rates jumped in 2022 and the aggregate bond index lost about 13%, its worst year on record.
- An inverted yield curve came before every recession since 1955, but it's no timing tool.
- T-Bills suit short-term money; TIPS and I Bonds hedge inflation, each with limits.
