## A bond is an IOU

A stock makes you an owner. A bond makes you a **lender**: the issuer borrows from you, promises to pay interest on schedule and repays the principal at maturity.

Every bond has three basics:

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Face value (par) | Principal repaid at maturity | $1,000 |
| Coupon rate | Annual interest as a percent of face value | 4% |
| Maturity | When the principal is repaid | In 10 years |

For the bond above, annual interest is 1,000 × 4% = $40. US bonds usually pay twice a year, so $20 each time. Over 10 years you collect $400 in interest and get $1,000 back at the end.

As long as the issuer doesn't default, the cash flows to maturity are fixed the day you buy. That's the biggest difference from stocks, where neither dividends nor prices are promised.

## Coupon and yield are different things

After issue, bonds trade in the market and their prices move. The coupon is based on face value and never changes; your actual return depends on the price you pay.

Say the bond above now trades at $950:

- You still collect $40 a year in interest.
- At maturity you get $1,000, $50 more than you paid.

So your annual return is above 4%. The annual return that counts both the interest and the gain or loss at maturity is the **yield to maturity (YTM)**. When comparing bonds or bond funds, compare yields, not coupons.

Why prices move is the subject of Lesson 15.

## Who issues bonds

| Type | Issuer | Credit risk | Tax on interest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Treasuries | US federal government | Usually treated as the lowest among dollar assets | Federal tax; exempt from state and local income tax |
| Municipal bonds | States, cities, school districts | Mostly low, varies by issuer | Usually exempt from federal income tax; often from state tax too if issued by your state |
| Corporate bonds | Companies | Varies widely; check the rating | Federal and state tax |

Treasuries come in three groups by maturity:

| Name | Maturity | How interest is paid |
|---|---|---|
| T-Bills | 4, 6, 8, 13, 17, 26, 52 weeks | Bought at a discount, repaid at face value; the difference is the interest |
| T-Notes | 2, 3, 5, 7, 10 years | Every six months |
| T-Bonds | 20, 30 years | Every six months |

You can buy Treasuries through a broker or directly from the US Treasury at TreasuryDirect.

::: tip Muni tax benefits depend on you
Munis usually pay less than corporate bonds of similar credit quality because their interest is usually free of federal income tax. The higher your tax rate, the more that's worth. In a low bracket, or inside a tax-advantaged account such as an IRA, the benefit is wasted.
:::

## Credit ratings

An issuer may fail to pay; that's a **default**. Rating agencies grade bonds with **credit ratings**. The three main agencies are S&P, Moody's and Fitch.

| | S&P / Fitch | Moody's |
|---|---|---|
| Highest | AAA | Aaa |
| Lowest investment grade | BBB- | Baa3 |
| Below that | BB+ and lower | Ba1 and lower |

- **Investment grade**: BBB- / Baa3 and above.
- **High-yield**: below investment grade, also called speculative grade or junk. The higher interest is pay for a higher chance of default.

A rating is the agency's opinion, not a guarantee. Ratings can be cut, and a bond's price usually falls when that happens.

## The main risks

- **Interest rate risk**: when rates rise, existing bond prices fall. The longer the maturity, the bigger the effect (Lesson 15).
- **Credit risk**: the issuer defaults or gets downgraded.
- **Inflation risk**: interest is fixed, so high inflation erodes the buying power of both interest and principal.
- **Call risk**: some bonds let the issuer repay early (callable bonds). Issuers tend to do this when rates fall, leaving you to reinvest at lower rates.

## Individual bonds or a bond fund

| | Individual bond | Bond fund / ETF |
|---|---|---|
| Maturity | Yes; hold to maturity and get face value | None; the fund keeps buying new bonds |
| Diversification | Needs many bonds and more money | One fund holds hundreds or thousands |
| Price | Sell early at the market price | Moves with the market every day |
| Best for | A known sum needed on a known date | The bond part of a long-term portfolio |

A bond fund has no maturity date, so there's no promise of getting your principal back. When rates rise its price falls, but the new bonds it buys pay more, so its income gradually rises.

For most beginners, a **total bond market index fund** from Lesson 6 (such as BND or AGG) is the simplest choice: low cost, holding Treasuries, corporate bonds and mortgage-backed securities, mostly investment grade. Check three things:

1. **Expense ratio** (Lesson 7).
2. **Average duration**: how sensitive it is to rates, covered in Lesson 15.
3. **Credit quality**: mostly investment grade, or mostly high-yield.

## What bonds do in a portfolio

Lesson 8 called stocks the growth engine and bonds the shock absorber. High-quality bonds usually swing less than stocks and often fall less when stocks crash.

That doesn't hold every time. In 2022, rates rose fast and stocks and bonds fell together. Lesson 15 explains why.

## Summary

- A bond is an IOU: face value, coupon and maturity set its cash flows.
- The coupon is fixed; the **yield** depends on the price you pay.
- Treasuries, munis and corporates differ in credit risk and tax; Treasury interest is free of state tax.
- **BBB-** (S&P/Fitch) and **Baa3** (Moody's) are the lowest investment-grade ratings; below that is high-yield.
- Bond funds have no maturity date; for most beginners, one low-cost total bond market index fund is enough.
