## Why read financial statements

Public companies must publish financial reports regularly. The stock price changes every day; the reports show how the business is actually doing.

At the core are three statements:

| Statement | Question it answers | Covers |
|---|---|---|
| Income statement | How much did it earn? | A period (quarter or year) |
| Balance sheet | What does it own and owe? | A single date |
| Cash flow statement | How much cash actually came in and went out? | A period |

We'll use a **hypothetical company**, Maple Coffee. All figures are in millions of dollars.

## Income statement: what it earned

| Item | Amount | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 1,000 | Money from selling coffee |
| − Cost of goods sold | 600 | Beans, store staff, etc. |
| **= Gross profit** | **400** | Gross margin 40% |
| − Selling, general and administrative | 200 | Rent, advertising, head office |
| − Research and development | 50 | |
| **= Operating income** | **150** | Operating margin 15% |
| − Interest expense | 10 | Interest on borrowing |
| − Income tax | 30 | |
| **= Net income** | **110** | Net margin 11% |
| Shares outstanding | 100 | |
| **Earnings per share (EPS)** | **$1.10** | 110 ÷ 100 |

Read it top to bottom: each dollar of revenue has costs taken out layer by layer, and net income is what's left.

Common ratios:

- **Gross margin** = gross profit ÷ revenue. Is the product itself profitable?
- **Operating margin** = operating income ÷ revenue. Is the core business profitable after running costs?
- **Net margin** = net income ÷ revenue.
- **EPS** = net income ÷ shares outstanding. Lesson 12 uses it for the P/E ratio.

One year's numbers mean little on their own. Compare them with the company's **past years** and with **competitors**.

## Balance sheet: what it owns and owes

The balance sheet is a snapshot on one date, and it always satisfies:

**Assets = liabilities + shareholders' equity**

| Assets | Amount | Liabilities and equity | Amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | 150 | Accounts payable | 100 |
| Accounts receivable | 120 | Short-term debt | 50 |
| Inventory | 80 | Long-term debt | 250 |
| Property and equipment | 650 | **Total liabilities** | **400** |
| | | **Shareholders' equity** | **600** |
| **Total assets** | **1,000** | **Total** | **1,000** |

- **Accounts receivable**: what customers have bought but not yet paid for.
- **Accounts payable**: what the company has bought but not yet paid suppliers.
- **Shareholders' equity**: assets minus liabilities, the part that belongs to shareholders; also called book value.

Two ratios to know:

- **Debt-to-equity** = liabilities ÷ equity = 400 ÷ 600 ≈ 0.67. Higher means more borrowing and more risk when business turns bad. It varies a lot by industry; banks and utilities naturally carry more debt.
- **Return on equity (ROE)** = net income ÷ equity = 110 ÷ 600 ≈ 18%. How much each dollar of shareholders' money earned in a year.

## Cash flow statement: did the cash show up

Net income is not the same as cash received. If a customer buys on credit, the revenue is recorded before the money arrives. The cash flow statement splits actual cash movements into three parts:

| Item | Amount | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Net income | 110 | From the income statement |
| + Depreciation | 60 | An expense, but no cash paid this year |
| − Increase in receivables | 40 | Revenue recorded, cash not received |
| − Increase in inventory | 10 | Cash spent on goods not yet sold |
| **Cash from operations** | **120** | |
| Buying equipment, opening stores (capital expenditure) | −100 | |
| **Cash from investing** | **−100** | |
| Repaying debt | −20 | |
| Paying dividends | −30 | |
| **Cash from financing** | **−50** | |
| **Net change in cash** | **−30** | |

A widely used measure is **free cash flow**:

**Free cash flow = cash from operations − capital expenditure = 120 − 100 = 20**

It's the cash left after the company maintains and expands the business. Maple Coffee earned 110 but generated only 20 in free cash flow, mostly because it's opening stores. That isn't bad in itself, but you should know how far profit and cash differ, and why.

::: warning Signs worth a closer look
- Net income far above operating cash flow, year after year.
- Receivables growing much faster than revenue.
- Debt rising every year while profit doesn't.
- "One-time" charges that show up every year.
:::

## Where to find the reports

US public companies file their reports with the SEC, and you can read them free in the SEC's **EDGAR** database. Company websites usually post them under "Investor Relations" too.

| Report | Contents | Deadline (large companies) |
|---|---|---|
| **10-K** | Annual report: audited financial statements, business description, risks | 60 days after fiscal year-end |
| **10-Q** | Quarterly report for each of the first three quarters; statements not audited | 40 days after quarter-end |
| **8-K** | Major events: executive changes, acquisitions, earnings releases | Within a few business days |

Smaller companies get more time: 75 or 90 days for the 10-K, 40 or 45 days for the 10-Q.

A 10-K is long. Start with three sections, **Business**, **Risk Factors** and **Management's Discussion and Analysis (MD&A)**, then the three statements.

::: info Watch "adjusted" numbers
Earnings releases often highlight "adjusted" (non-GAAP) earnings, which usually leave out items such as stock-based compensation and restructuring costs. No common standard governs these figures, and each company calculates them its own way. Compare them with GAAP net income.
:::

## Summary

- The **income statement** shows earnings, the **balance sheet** shows what's owned and owed, and the **cash flow statement** shows actual cash in and out.
- Assets = liabilities + shareholders' equity.
- Profit isn't cash; **free cash flow** is operating cash flow minus capital expenditure.
- Reports are free on the SEC's EDGAR: 10-K annual, 10-Q quarterly, 8-K for major events.
- Compare the numbers with the company's past and with its peers.
