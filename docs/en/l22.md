## Investors often earn less than their funds

Each year Morningstar compares fund returns with what investors actually earned. Its 2026 edition covers the ten years ended December 31, 2025:

| | Annual return |
|---|---|
| Total return of US funds | 9.9% |
| Average return on the money invested in them | 8.7% |
| Gap | About 1.2 percentage points a year |

The gap comes from timing. Fund returns assume you held all along; investors' money tends to arrive after gains and leave after losses.

1.2 points a year doesn't sound like much. $10,000 growing at 9.9% for ten years reaches about $25,700; at 8.7%, about $23,000, roughly $2,700 less.

## Common decision mistakes

### Loss aversion and chasing

Losing money hurts more than gaining the same amount feels good. That's **loss aversion**.

It pushes people to sell in a panic to "stop the bleeding." As Lesson 2 showed, the S&P 500 fell about 34% in 33 days in February–March 2020, then got back to its previous high that August. Those who sold in March locked in the loss and missed the rebound.

The flip side: when others are making money and rallies fill the news, people pile in near the top for fear of missing out (FOMO).

### Overconfidence and overtrading

Most people think their judgment is better than average. A few wins make it easy to mistake luck for skill, and trading picks up.

What frequent trading costs:

- Every trade pays the bid-ask spread (Lesson 10).
- Short-term gains are taxed at ordinary rates, higher than long-term rates (Lesson 21).
- More trades mean more chances to make a bad call.

As Lesson 6 showed, most professional active fund managers trail the index over the long run.

### Anchoring

Using an irrelevant number as a reference point. The most common is your **purchase price**: "I'll sell when it gets back to what I paid." The stock doesn't know what you paid. Ask instead: if you had cash today, would you buy it?

### Recency bias

Assuming whatever just happened will continue. After several up years, it feels like stocks will keep rising; right after a crash, it feels like they'll keep falling.

### Herding

If everyone around you is buying something, it feels safe. By the time a crowd is buying, the price has often already risen a lot.

## Why market timing is hard

Timing requires being right twice in a row:

1. Selling before the drop.
2. Buying back before the rebound.

Getting only the first right, avoiding a drop but not buying back in time, often leaves you worse off than holding. The biggest up days often come right after the biggest down days, so waiting until "things are clearer" often means missing the rebound.

## Managing risk: rules instead of in-the-moment calls

Willpower rarely beats these biases. Rules set in advance work better:

- **Emergency fund first** (Lesson 3): you won't be forced to sell at a low to cover a bill.
- **Invest only long-term money**: money needed within a few years belongs in cash, T-Bills or something similarly stable.
- **Diversify**: index funds instead of bets on a few stocks (Lessons 2 and 6).
- **Cap single positions**: set a limit for any one stock, such as 5%–10% of your total. The right number varies; the point is to set it in advance.
- **No leverage**: margin and futures can turn swings into losses you can't absorb (Lesson 20).
- **Automate**: automatic investing and rebalancing (Lesson 8) cut the number of decisions you make.
- **Write a plan**: Lesson 23.

## Spotting scams

Scammers target exactly these instincts: greed, fear of missing out and trust in people you know. Common red flags:

- **Guaranteed high returns**, "can't lose." Every real investment has risk.
- **Pressure to act now**: "limited spots," "ends today."
- **Unsolicited contact**, or "trading mentors" and "inside tips" on social media or in chat groups.
- **A friendship or romance that turns to investing**, often in crypto or an unfamiliar platform.
- **Requests to send money to a personal account** or install an unknown trading app.
- **Rewards for recruiting others**.

::: warning Check before you send money
In the US, people and firms that sell securities or give investment advice generally must be registered with the SEC, FINRA or a state regulator. You can look up registration and disciplinary history on the SEC's investor.gov and FINRA's BrokerCheck. If you can't find them, don't send money.
:::

## Summary

- Investors often earn less than the funds they own, mainly from **buying high and selling low**.
- Common biases: loss aversion, overconfidence, anchoring, recency and herding.
- Market timing means being right twice in a row, which is hard to do consistently.
- Manage risk with rules: emergency fund, diversification, position caps, no leverage, automation.
- Guaranteed returns, pressure to transfer money and stock tips in chat groups are scam signs; check registration before sending money.
