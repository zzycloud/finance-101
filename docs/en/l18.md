All examples use Maple Coffee, the hypothetical company from Lesson 11, now at $50. Each contract is 100 shares; fees are ignored and profits are measured at expiration.

## Covered calls

**How**: own 100 shares and sell one call against them.

You buy 100 shares at $50 and sell one $55 call expiring in a month for a $1 premium, or $100.

| Stock at expiration | Stock P/L | Option P/L | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| $40 | −$1,000 | +$100 | −$900 |
| $50 | $0 | +$100 | +$100 |
| $55 | +$500 | +$100 | +$600 |
| $70 | +$2,000 | −$1,400 | +$600 |

- If the stock is flat or rises a little, you earn an extra $100.
- Above $55, your shares are called away at $55 and you make at most $600. At $70, you miss $1,400 of gains.
- If the stock falls, the premium offsets only $1 of the drop; the rest of the loss is the same as just owning the shares.

**In short**: you trade upside for a fixed income, and your downside barely changes.

## Protective puts

**How**: own 100 shares and buy one put, like insurance on the stock.

You buy 100 shares at $50 and pay $1.50 ($150) for one $45 put.

| Stock at expiration | Stock P/L | Option P/L | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| $30 | −$2,000 | +$1,350 | −$650 |
| $45 | −$500 | −$150 | −$650 |
| $50 | $0 | −$150 | −$150 |
| $70 | +$2,000 | −$150 | +$1,850 |

- However far the stock falls, you lose at most $650 before expiration.
- The cost is the premium. If the stock doesn't fall, the $150 was the insurance bill.
- Options expire. Staying protected means buying new puts again and again, which can cost a lot each year and noticeably drag on long-term returns.

## Cash-secured puts

**How**: sell a put and keep enough cash in the account to buy the shares at the strike.

You sell one $45 put for $150 and set aside $4,500.

- Stock above $45 at expiration: the put expires and you keep $150.
- Stock at $40: you must buy 100 shares at $45. After the premium, your cost is $43.50, a $350 paper loss.
- Worst case: the stock goes to 0 and you lose $4,350.

**In short**: you take a premium in exchange for the obligation to buy at $45 if the stock falls there. The risk is close to buying the stock at $43.50, with upside capped at $150.

## Spreads

**How**: buy and sell options of the same type at once. The premium from the one you sell lowers your cost and caps both your gain and your loss.

**Bull call spread**: buy a $50 call ($3), sell a $55 call ($1), paying $2 net, or $200.

| Stock at expiration | Total P/L |
|---|---|
| $50 or lower | −$200 |
| $52 | $0 |
| $55 or higher | +$300 |

You lose at most $200 and make at most $300. It's cheaper than buying the call alone, but the gain is capped.

## Why beginners often lose with options

1. **You need the direction, size and timing right.** A stock only has to rise eventually; an option has to rise enough before expiration.
2. **Time decay**: time value shrinks every day, faster as expiration nears (Lesson 17).
3. **Small bets often go to zero**: a few hundred dollars of out-of-the-money options commonly expire worthless, a 100% loss.
4. **IV drops**: buying before earnings or other events, you can lose after the event even if you got the direction right (Lesson 17).
5. **Wide bid-ask spreads**: many options trade thinly, and the spread can be a large share of the price, so each round trip costs a lot.
6. **Sellers can lose big**: a naked call's loss has no limit; a short put loses heavily in a crash. The SEC's investor.gov warns that you can lose your entire investment trading options, and sometimes more.
7. **Same-day options (0DTE)**: time value goes to zero within hours and prices swing extremely fast. It's close to gambling.

::: warning Assignment and automatic exercise
- If you sell American-style options, you can be assigned at any time before expiration; short calls, for example, may be exercised early before an ex-dividend date.
- In-the-money options are usually exercised automatically at expiration. If you hold an in-the-money call without enough cash for 100 shares, you may end up with a margin loan or your broker may step in. Check your broker's rules.
:::

## How a beginner should think about options

- Long-term index fund investors don't need options.
- If you want to try, use only covered calls or protective puts **on shares you already own**, and know your worst case first.
- Don't sell naked options, don't buy options with borrowed money and don't buy short-dated out-of-the-money options because they look cheap.

## Summary

- **Covered call**: trade upside for premium; downside barely changes.
- **Protective put**: pay for insurance that caps your loss; not cheap over time.
- **Cash-secured put**: risk close to buying the stock at a lower price.
- **Spreads**: lower the cost and cap both gain and loss.
- Beginners mostly lose to time decay, IV drops, spread costs and the large losses sellers can take.
