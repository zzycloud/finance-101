## Where stocks trade

US stocks are listed mainly on two exchanges:

- **New York Stock Exchange (NYSE)**
- **Nasdaq**

Individuals can't send orders to an exchange directly; they go through a **broker**. You place an order in your broker's app, and the broker routes it to an exchange or another trading venue (Lesson 4 covers opening an account).

For a long-term investor, which exchange a stock is listed on makes almost no difference.

## Trading hours

Regular trading hours are **9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern Time**, Monday to Friday, closed on US market holidays.

Many brokers also offer **pre-market** and **after-hours** trading. Fewer people trade then, and the SEC has warned about the risks: lower volume, wider spreads and sharper price moves. Many brokers accept only limit orders outside regular hours. Beginners can stick to regular hours.

## Bid, ask and spread

As Lesson 7 explained, there are always two prices:

- **Bid**: the highest price someone will pay right now, which is what you get if you sell.
- **Ask**: the lowest price someone will sell at right now, which is what you pay if you buy.

The gap is the **spread**. Large, heavily traded stocks often have a one-cent spread; small, thinly traded ones can have much wider spreads. You pay this hidden cost on every trade.

## Four common order types

| Order | How it executes | Benefit | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market | Fills right away at the best available price | Almost certain to fill | Price may be worse than expected |
| Limit | Fills only at your price or better | Price is guaranteed | May not fill |
| Stop | Becomes a market order once the stop price is reached | Caps losses in a decline | Fill can be far below the stop price |
| Stop-limit | Becomes a limit order once the stop price is reached | Fill price has a floor | May not sell in a fast drop |

### A stop price is not a guaranteed price

Say you own a $100 stock and set a stop order at $90.

- If the stock drifts down to $90, the order triggers and you sell near $90.
- If the company announces bad news overnight and the stock opens at $80, the order triggers and fills at the market, around $80, not $90.

With a stop-limit order instead (stop $90, limit $88), a jump to $80 triggers the order, but the market is below $88, so it doesn't fill and you still own the stock.

The SEC's investor bulletin warns about both risks. A stop order does not guarantee that your loss stops at 10%.

### How long an order lasts

You usually also choose a duration:

- **Day**: cancelled automatically if not filled by the close.
- **Good 'til canceled (GTC)**: stays open until it fills or you cancel it. Brokers set a maximum life.

If you leave a GTC limit order open, remember it's there. A swing weeks later can fill it when it no longer matches what you want.

::: tip How a beginner can place orders
For large stocks or big ETFs, trade during regular hours with a **limit order** set near the current ask (when buying) or bid (when selling). It will usually fill right away, and a sudden price jump can't fill you at an unreasonable price.
:::

## Settlement: T+1

After a trade, the formal exchange of shares and cash is called **settlement**.

- Since **May 28, 2024**, US stocks settle **T+1**: one business day after the trade.
- From 2017 until then, it was T+2.

The practical effect for you: money from a sale becomes available sooner.

## Fractional shares

Some brokers let you buy **fractional shares**, such as 0.1 of a share, or "$50 of this stock". That lets small amounts buy high-priced stocks. Rules vary by broker, and some don't let you transfer fractional shares to another broker.

## Circuit breakers

When the S&P 500 falls too far in one day, trading stops across the whole market. These are **market-wide circuit breakers**:

| Level | Drop from prior close | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 7% | Before 3:25 p.m. ET, market-wide 15-minute halt |
| 2 | 13% | Before 3:25 p.m. ET, market-wide 15-minute halt |
| 3 | 20% | At any time, trading stops for the rest of the day |

Levels 1 and 2 don't halt trading if hit at or after 3:25 p.m. Circuit breakers give the market time to absorb information; they don't prevent declines. In March 2020, Level 1 was triggered four times in less than two weeks.

## Summary

- You trade through a broker; the listing exchange barely matters to a long-term investor.
- Regular hours are 9:30 a.m.–4:00 p.m. ET; extended hours are thinner, and beginners can skip them.
- Use **limit orders** to control your price; a stop price is **not a guaranteed price**.
- US stocks have settled **T+1** since May 28, 2024.
