## The "lazy" approach

Say you want to invest in the US stock market. There are two ways to think about it:

1. **Pick stocks**: research which companies will win and buy only those.
2. **Buy everything**: don't guess the winners; just buy the whole market.

The first sounds smarter and the second sounds lazy. But decades of data show that **for most people, the "lazy" way does better over the long run.** That's the **index fund**.

Vanguard founder John Bogle launched the first index mutual fund for ordinary investors in 1976. His famous line:

> **Don't look for the needle in the haystack. Just buy the haystack.**

## What an index is

An **index** is a rules-based list of stocks used to measure how a market is doing, like a class's "average score" that tells you how the whole class did.

The most common ones:

| Index | What's in it | In one line |
|---|---|---|
| **S&P 500** | About 500 large US companies | The usual stand-in for "the US stock market" |
| **US total market** (e.g., CRSP US Total Market) | Thousands of US companies, large to small | Broader than the S&P 500 |
| **Nasdaq-100** | About 100 of the largest non-financial companies listed on Nasdaq | Heavy in tech, highly concentrated |
| **Global ex-US** (e.g., FTSE Global All Cap ex US) | Developed and emerging markets outside the US | For international diversification |

You can't buy an index directly. An **index fund** buys the stocks on the list so you can "own the index."

## Market-cap weighting: bigger companies count more

The S&P 500 doesn't give each company 1/500. It weights by **market capitalization**:

> **Market cap = share price × shares outstanding**, i.e., what it would cost to buy the whole company.

The bigger the company, the bigger its weight. In recent years, the ten largest companies in the S&P 500 have made up more than 30% of the index.

One benefit of this design: **little trading is needed**. When a company grows, its weight rises automatically, without the fund buying or selling. That's one reason index funds are cheap to run.

::: info Something to think about
Market-cap weighting means that when a handful of giants (like a few big tech companies) do especially well, the S&P 500 leans heavily on them. That has helped recent returns, and it's also a concentration risk worth understanding.
:::

## Why most active funds lag the index

S&P Dow Jones Indices publishes the **SPIVA** report every year, comparing active funds with their benchmarks. In 2025, about **79%** of actively managed US large-cap funds lagged the S&P 500. Over longer windows like 10 or 15 years, the share that lag is typically even higher.

These are professionals. Why can't they win? Three main reasons:

1. **Fees**: active funds cost more because research and trading cost money. An index fund might charge 0.03% a year; 0.5% to 1% is common for active funds.
2. **Simple arithmetic**: all investors together *are* the market. Before costs, the average active investor earns the market return. After higher costs, the average active investor **must** trail it. This point is from Nobel laureate William Sharpe.
3. **Winning consistently is hard**: this year's winners often lose next year. Picking future winning funds in advance is about as hard as picking stocks.

## The long-run power of fees

Last lesson showed expense ratios look small. Here's what they do with compounding: $100,000 invested, 7% gross return a year, held 30 years.

| Expense ratio | After 30 years, roughly |
|---|---|
| 0.03% | $754,800 |
| 0.50% | $661,400 |
| 1.00% | $574,300 |

Between 0.03% and 1% is a gap of about **$180,000**, collected by the fund company whether it performs well or not.

::: tip You control very little in investing
You can't control the market or the economy, but you **can control costs**. Choosing low-cost funds is the most reliable "edge" an ordinary investor has.
:::

## Common index funds

These are well-known examples, **for illustration only, not recommendations**. The same index is often offered by several companies; the differences are mostly cost and platform.

| Index tracked | ETF examples | Mutual fund examples |
|---|---|---|
| S&P 500 | VOO (Vanguard), IVV (iShares), SPY (State Street) | FXAIX (Fidelity), SWPPX (Schwab), VFIAX (Vanguard) |
| US total market | VTI (Vanguard) | FSKAX (Fidelity), VTSAX (Vanguard) |
| International stocks | VXUS (Vanguard) | FTIHX (Fidelity), VTIAX (Vanguard) |
| US aggregate bonds | BND (Vanguard), AGG (iShares) | FXNAX (Fidelity), VBTLX (Vanguard) |

A fee comparison: as reported in June 2026, among S&P 500 ETFs, VOO's expense ratio was 0.03% and SPY's about 0.09%. They hold nearly identical stocks, so for long-term holders the cheaper one wins. (SPY's huge trading volume appeals to short-term traders and institutions, which matters little to long-term investors.)

## Limits of index funds

Index funds aren't a sure thing. Know their limits:

- **They fall with the market**: in the 2007–2009 financial crisis, the S&P 500 fell more than half from its peak, and so did the funds tracking it. The goal is the market's return, not avoiding losses.
- **You won't beat the market**: you'll never have the best return in the room, but you'll rarely have the worst.
- **The S&P 500 alone isn't full diversification**: it's only large US companies, with no small companies, international stocks or bonds. Combining them is Lesson 8.

## Summary

- An **index** is a list that measures a market; an **index fund** buys the whole list.
- The **S&P 500** is about 500 large US companies, weighted by market cap.
- Most active funds **lag their index** over time, mainly because of costs.
- A 1% difference in fees can cost six figures over decades.
- Index funds rise and fall with the market; they aim for the market's return, not for "no losses".
