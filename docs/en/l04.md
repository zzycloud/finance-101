## Accounts are containers

A common beginner mix-up: an **account** and an **investment** are different things.

- An **account** is the container: a taxable brokerage account, an IRA, a 401(k).
- An **investment** is what goes inside: funds, stocks, bonds.

The same fund can sit in different accounts. The account type decides **how it's taxed**, **how much you can put in** and **when you can take money out**. Choosing the right container can save a lot of tax over time.

## What's a broker? Is my money safe there?

A **broker** is the company that lets you buy and sell stocks and funds. Common US examples include Fidelity, Charles Schwab and Vanguard (examples, not recommendations). Most large brokers now charge no commission to trade US stocks and ETFs.

What if the broker goes under?

The **SIPC (Securities Investor Protection Corporation)** steps in when a brokerage firm fails and customer assets are missing. It helps restore them, within limits:

- Up to **$500,000** per customer, including up to **$250,000** in cash.
- It covers securities such as stocks, bonds, mutual funds and ETFs.

::: warning What SIPC does not cover
SIPC does **not** protect you from market losses. If your stock falls by half, that's investment risk, not a broker failure. It also doesn't cover commodity futures or most crypto assets.
:::

Also, regulated brokers must keep customer assets separate from the firm's own, so a broker failing is not the same as your assets disappearing.

## Account types at a glance

| Account | In one line | Tax treatment | Can you withdraw any time? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Taxable brokerage | The most flexible | Tax on gains when you sell and on dividends | Yes |
| 401(k) | Employer retirement plan | Traditional: tax break now, taxed when withdrawn | Before 59½, usually a 10% penalty plus tax |
| Traditional IRA | Retirement account you open yourself | Possible tax break now, taxed when withdrawn | Same as above |
| Roth IRA | Retirement account you open yourself | After-tax money in, tax-free out if rules are met | Contributions any time; earnings with conditions |

Let's go through them.

## Taxable brokerage account

Simple and flexible: put in as much as you like, take it out whenever you want.

The trade-off is **tax**: you pay capital gains tax when you sell at a profit, and tax on dividends. Holding more than a year before selling usually gets you the lower long-term capital gains rate (Lesson 21).

Good for: medium- to long-term goals other than retirement, or money beyond what your retirement accounts allow.

## 401(k): your employer's retirement plan

A **401(k)** is a retirement account many US employers offer, funded straight from your paycheck.

Two big advantages:

1. **Tax benefits**: money in a traditional 401(k) isn't counted as taxable income that year. You pay less tax now and pay it later when you withdraw. Many plans also offer a Roth 401(k).
2. **Employer match**: many employers add money in proportion to what you put in.

How a match works: say your employer will "**match 50% of your contributions, up to 6% of salary**." On an $80,000 salary:

- You contribute 6%: $4,800
- Employer adds 50% of that: **$2,400**

That $2,400 is a 50% return in year one, for free. So a very common rule is: **contribute at least enough to get the full match.**

::: info Watch for vesting
Employer contributions sometimes only become fully yours after you've worked there a certain number of years. This is called vesting. Your own contributions are always yours.
:::

**Contribution limits** (your own contributions, from IRS announcements):

| | 2026 | 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| 401(k) employee limit | $24,500 | $25,500 |
| Extra catch-up, age 50+ | $8,000 | $8,500 |
| Extra catch-up, ages 60–63 (most plans) | $11,250 | $11,750 |

## IRA: a retirement account you open yourself

An **IRA (Individual Retirement Account)** doesn't depend on an employer; you open one at a broker. The two main kinds are the **traditional IRA** and the **Roth IRA**.

To contribute, you need **taxable compensation** (earned income) for the year, and you can't contribute more than that amount.

**Contribution limits** (combined across both kinds of IRA):

| | 2026 | 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| IRA limit | $7,500 | $7,500 |
| Extra catch-up, age 50+ | $1,100 | $1,100 |

You can generally make contributions for a given year up until that year's tax filing deadline (usually around April 15 of the next year).

### Traditional vs. Roth IRA

The core difference is one thing: **when you pay tax.**

| | Traditional IRA | Roth IRA |
|---|---|---|
| Going in | May be tax-deductible (limited if your income is high and you have a workplace plan) | After-tax money, no deduction |
| While it grows | No tax | No tax |
| Withdrawals in retirement | Taxed as ordinary income | **Tax-free** if rules are met |
| Income limits | None to contribute (limits on deducting) | Above a certain income, you can't contribute directly |

**Roth IRA income limits** (based on modified adjusted gross income, MAGI; within the range your allowed contribution shrinks, and above it you can't contribute directly):

| Filing status | 2026 | 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| Single | $153,000–$168,000 | $158,000–$173,000 |
| Married filing jointly | $242,000–$252,000 | $250,000–$260,000 |

**Which to choose?** A simple way to think about it:

- If you expect your **tax rate now to be lower than in retirement** (say, early in your career), Roth is often better: pay a little tax now, nothing later.
- If you **earn a lot now and face a high tax rate**, the upfront tax break of a traditional IRA or 401(k) is more attractive.

If you're unsure, using a bit of both is common.

::: tip Roth IRA flexibility
The **contributions** you put into a Roth IRA can be taken out any time, tax- and penalty-free. Only the **earnings** need to meet conditions (generally age 59½ and the account open at least five years) to come out tax-free. That makes a Roth more flexible than many people think, though it still shouldn't be your emergency fund.
:::

## A common saving order

Building on the last lesson, a typical order is:

1. 401(k) up to the full employer match
2. Clear high-interest debt and fill the emergency fund
3. Fund an IRA (many younger people start with a Roth IRA)
4. Go back and raise 401(k) contributions
5. Anything left goes to a taxable brokerage account

## If you're outside the US

This course focuses on US markets, but readers come from everywhere. A few notes:

- Many US brokers accept non-US residents. You'll usually fill in a **W-8BEN** form stating your tax status.
- For non-US tax residents, US dividends are withheld at **30%** by default. If your country has a tax treaty with the US, the rate may be lower (for example, generally **10%** for residents of mainland China).
- IRAs and 401(k)s require US taxable compensation, so most non-residents can't use them.
- Your own country may have its own tax and currency rules for foreign investing. Check them carefully.

Tax situations vary. For specifics, rely on official rules or a qualified tax professional.

## Summary

- **Accounts are containers, investments are contents**; the account type decides the tax.
- **SIPC** protects your assets if a broker fails (up to $500,000), not from market drops.
- **401(k)**: get the full employer match first. It's free money.
- **Traditional vs. Roth**: pay tax now or later.
- Contribution limits change yearly; the IRS figures are the source of truth.
