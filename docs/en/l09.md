## What a share is

A company's ownership is divided into many equal pieces. Each piece is a **share**. Buy one and you own a small part of the company.

If a company has 100 million shares and you hold 100, you own one millionth of it. As a shareholder you can:

- **Share in profits**: when the company pays a dividend, you're paid per share (Lesson 13).
- **Vote**: on matters such as electing directors, usually one vote per share.
- **Share in growth**: if the company becomes more valuable, your shares usually do too.

You don't run the business and you aren't liable for its debts. If the company goes bankrupt, the most you can lose is what you paid for the shares.

::: tip Stocks vs. funds
The index funds in Lessons 5–8 buy hundreds of companies at once. A single stock depends entirely on one company, so the risk is far more concentrated.
:::

## Market cap: size is not the share price

Beginners often read a high share price as a big company, as if a $500 stock were "bigger" than a $50 one. It isn't.

**Market cap = share price × shares outstanding**

| | Share price | Shares outstanding | Market cap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Company A | $500 | 10 million | $5 billion |
| Company B | $50 | 1 billion | $50 billion |

A's share price is 10 times B's, but its market cap is one tenth. The share price only reflects how many pieces the ownership is cut into.

## Why companies go public

When a company sells shares to the public for the first time, it's an **initial public offering (IPO)**. After that its shares are listed on an exchange and anyone can trade them.

Companies go public mainly to:

1. **Raise money** for expansion, research or paying down debt.
2. **Let early owners sell**: founders, employees and early investors can sell their shares in the market.

The cost: a public company must publish financial reports regularly and is regulated by the SEC. Those reports are the main way individual investors learn about a company (Lesson 11 shows how to read them).

::: warning A new listing is not a reason to buy
Big price swings on IPO day are common. A newly listed company has little public history, so whether the price is reasonable is hard to judge. Don't buy a stock just because it's new and popular.
:::

## Why prices move

On an exchange, the price is set by buyers and sellers. More buyers push it up; more sellers push it down.

What drives them? Over the long run, mostly **expectations of what the company will earn**.

- If profits keep growing, the price usually follows.
- What matters is **results compared with expectations**. Profit up 20% when the market expected 30% can still send the stock down.
- Interest rates, the economy, industry news and investor mood all shift expectations.

In the short run a stock can swing on a headline that has little to do with the business. Over the long run, the price tends to track the company's actual earning power. Benjamin Graham's version: in the short run the market is a voting machine; in the long run it is a weighing machine.

## Common vs. preferred stock

| | Common | Preferred |
|---|---|---|
| Voting rights | Yes, usually one vote per share | Usually none |
| Dividends | Not fixed; can be skipped | Usually fixed |
| Order of payment | Last | Before common |
| Shares in growth | Yes | Rarely |

Preferred stock sits between a stock and a bond. What most people buy in a brokerage app is **common stock**.

Some companies issue more than one class of common stock, such as Class A and Class B, usually differing in voting rights. Alphabet (Google's parent), for example, trades as GOOGL, which votes, and GOOG, which doesn't. Check which class a ticker refers to before you buy.

## Summary

- A share is a small piece of ownership; the most you can lose is what you paid.
- Judge a company's size by **market cap**, not share price.
- Companies go public to raise money and let early owners sell; in return they must disclose their finances.
- Over the long run prices follow **earnings expectations**; in the short run news and mood move them a lot.
- Most individual investors hold common stock; preferred stock usually pays a fixed dividend and has no vote.
