## A great company is not always a great investment

Buying the same company at $20 or at $200 gives very different results. **Valuation** is judging whether the price is cheap or expensive relative to what the company earns.

We'll keep using Maple Coffee, the hypothetical company from Lesson 11:

| | Value |
|---|---|
| Share price | $22 |
| Shares outstanding | 100 million |
| Market cap | $2.2 billion |
| Annual revenue | $1 billion |
| Net income | $110 million |
| Earnings per share (EPS) | $1.10 |
| Shareholders' equity | $600 million, $6 per share |
| Annual dividend | $0.30 per share |

## Price-to-earnings (P/E)

**P/E = share price ÷ EPS = 22 ÷ 1.10 = 20**

One way to read it: if profits never changed, it would take 20 years of earnings to pay back the price.

Flip it and 1 ÷ 20 = 5% is the **earnings yield**: for every $100 invested, the company earns $5 a year on your behalf. You can compare that with Treasury yields.

Two common versions:

- **Trailing P/E** uses actual earnings over the past 12 months. The numbers are real but already in the past.
- **Forward P/E** uses analysts' forecasts for the next 12 months. It looks ahead, but forecasts can be wrong.

### What a high or low P/E tells you

A high P/E means the market will pay more for each dollar of earnings, usually because it expects earnings to grow fast.

| | Company A | Company B |
|---|---|---|
| Current P/E | 40 | 12 |
| Market expectation | Earnings grow 25% a year | Earnings roughly flat |

Company A looks expensive, but if its earnings grow as expected, its P/E based on future earnings will be much lower in a few years. If growth disappoints, high-P/E stocks tend to fall hard.

A low P/E isn't automatically cheap either. The company may face decline, lawsuits or a shrinking industry, and the low price reflects that.

::: tip Compare like with like
P/E ratios vary widely by industry; comparing a software company with a bank tells you nothing. Compare a company with its **peers** and with **its own history**.
:::

## Price-to-book and price-to-sales

**P/B = share price ÷ book value per share = 22 ÷ 6 ≈ 3.7**

Book value per share is shareholders' equity divided by shares. P/B is used mostly for banks, insurers and other businesses whose assets are largely financial. For tech companies, brands, patents and people mostly aren't on the balance sheet, so P/B says little.

**P/S = market cap ÷ annual revenue = 2.2 ÷ 1 = 2.2**

When a company loses money, P/E is meaningless, so P/S is used to compare companies that aren't yet profitable. Its weakness is that it ignores profit entirely: a company with big sales that never makes money can still have a low P/S.

## Dividend yield and payout ratio

**Dividend yield = annual dividend per share ÷ price = 0.30 ÷ 22 ≈ 1.4%**

**Payout ratio = dividends ÷ net income = 0.30 ÷ 1.10 ≈ 27%**

The payout ratio shows how much of its profit the company pays out. A payout ratio above 100% for years means the company pays more than it earns, which is hard to sustain.

::: warning Watch for the high-yield trap
Dividend yield = dividend ÷ price, so when the price falls, the yield rises on its own.

A company paying $2 a share yields 4% at $50. At $20, it yields 10%. That looks attractive, but a price drop that large often means something has gone wrong, and a dividend cut may come next. When a yield looks unusually high, find out why the price fell.
:::

## Limits of valuation

- **One year's earnings can mislead**: one-time gains or charges can push a single year's P/E too high or too low. Averages over several years are safer.
- **It doesn't predict short-term moves**: a cheap stock can keep falling, and an expensive one can keep rising for years.
- **Look at the business**: two companies at 20 times earnings, one growing and one shrinking, are worth very different amounts.
- **Professionals find it hard too**: as Lesson 6 showed, most actively managed funds trail the index over the long run.

Valuation tools help you **avoid obviously overpaying** and see what expectations are built into a price. If you mainly own index funds, this lesson's job is to help you follow news like "valuations are stretched" or "P/E hits a record".

## Summary

- **P/E** = price ÷ EPS; a high P/E means the market expects strong growth.
- **P/B** suits asset-heavy businesses such as banks; **P/S** works for companies not yet profitable.
- **Dividend yield** rises when the price falls; check why before trusting a high yield.
- Compare valuations with peers and with the company's history; they don't predict short-term moves.
