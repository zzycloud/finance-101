## A simple truth

If someone tells you, "This investment earns 20% a year, guaranteed, zero risk," be suspicious right away.

Markets have something close to an iron rule: **the higher the expected return, the more risk you usually take on.** High returns with no risk almost never exist, and many people who promise them are running scams.

This lesson covers three things: what risk really is, roughly where different assets sit, and how to manage risk sensibly.

## What risk really is

In everyday speech, "risk" means "I might lose money." In investing, it helps to split that into two kinds:

1. **Volatility**: prices jump around. Up 2% today, down 3% tomorrow. It feels bad on paper, but as long as you don't sell and the asset itself is sound, it can recover.
2. **Permanent loss**: the money is really gone. A company goes bankrupt and its stock goes to zero, or you panic-sell in a crash and turn a paper loss into a real one.

For long-term investors, **the second kind is the one to fear**. Many people lose money not because they bought the wrong thing, but because they couldn't sit through the swings and sold at the bottom.

## The risk-return ladder

Here's roughly where common assets sit. Going up, both risk and expected return rise:

| Asset | Rough character | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Options, futures, leveraged products | Can lose most of your money, or even more than you put in, very quickly | Very high |
| Individual stocks | One company can soar or go bankrupt | High |
| Stock index funds | Spread across hundreds or thousands of companies, but the whole market still swings | Medium-high |
| Bond funds | Smaller swings than stocks, but they fall when rates rise | Medium-low |
| Short-term US Treasury bills | Backed by the US government, short maturities, very stable prices | Low |
| Bank deposits, high-yield savings | FDIC-insured up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category | Very low (but may lose to inflation) |

This is only a rough map. Specific products vary, and later lessons cover each one.

## Big crashes in history

Over the long run stocks have grown, but the road is bumpy. Here are some well-known declines in the **S&P 500** (the "average score" of about 500 large US companies, covered in the funds chapter):

| Period | Event | Approximate peak-to-low drop |
|---|---|---|
| 2000–2002 | Dot-com bust | about 49% |
| 2007–2009 | Global financial crisis | about 57% |
| Feb–Mar 2020 | COVID shock | about 34% (in about a month) |
| 2022 | High inflation and rate hikes | about 25% |

If you had invested $100,000 at the 2007 peak, by early 2009 you'd have had about $43,000. Could you have held on without selling? Every investor needs to answer that honestly.

There's another bit of math many people miss: **losses and recoveries aren't symmetric.**

| Drop | Gain needed to break even |
|---|---|
| −10% | +11% |
| −20% | +25% |
| −30% | +43% |
| −50% | +100% |

That's why avoiding big losses matters more than chasing big gains.

## Diversification: the only free lunch

Nobel laureate Harry Markowitz's portfolio theory is often summed up in one line: **diversification is the only free lunch in investing.**

It means that by spreading money across many assets that don't move in lockstep, you can cut risk substantially without giving up much expected return.

A concrete example:

- You put everything into **one company**. It collapses in an accounting scandal or goes bankrupt (Enron and Lehman Brothers are historical examples). You could lose it all.
- You own an index fund holding **500 companies**. If one goes bankrupt, it's a tiny slice of your money.

Diversification works on several levels:

- **Across companies**: don't bet everything on one or two names.
- **Across industries**: tech, healthcare, consumer, energy… leadership rotates.
- **Across asset types**: stocks and bonds often don't move together.
- **Across countries**: there are markets outside the US.

::: warning Diversification isn't magic
It removes the risk of any single company failing, but not the risk of the whole market falling together. In 2008 almost every stock fell. To handle market-wide drops, you rely on **time horizon** and **asset allocation**, covered next.
:::

## Time and risk

In the short run the market is like a coin flip; in the long run it's more like a weighing machine. The longer you hold, the lower the chance you end up with a loss, because there's time to recover from declines.

So what you invest in depends heavily on **when you'll need the money**:

| When you need it | Better fit |
|---|---|
| Within 1 year | Savings account, money market fund, short-term Treasuries |
| 1–5 years | Mostly stable assets, maybe a little stock |
| 5–10+ years | Can hold more stock funds and use time to absorb the swings |

That's why the next lesson starts with getting your short-term money in order before you invest.

## Your "sleep at night" test

One very practical question: **if your investments fell 30% in a month, what would you do?**

- If you'd panic, lose sleep and want to sell everything, you're taking more risk than you can handle right now.
- If you'd calmly keep investing as planned, that risk level fits you.

The right amount of risk isn't the theoretical optimum. It's the amount you **can actually live with**.

## Summary

- High returns come with high risk; be wary of "guaranteed high returns".
- Risk comes in two kinds: **volatility** and **permanent loss**. The second is the dangerous one.
- Losses and recoveries aren't symmetric: a 50% drop needs a 100% gain to recover.
- **Diversification** sharply reduces the damage from any single company.
- When you need the money determines how much risk it can take.
