## What a company does with its profit

A profitable company has roughly three choices:

1. **Keep it in the business**: open stores, fund research, make acquisitions, pay down debt.
2. **Pay a cash dividend**: send money directly to shareholders.
3. **Buy back stock**: use the money to repurchase its own shares.

The last two return cash to shareholders. Many fast-growing companies pay little or no dividend and reinvest everything; mature companies are more likely to pay dividends and buy back stock. A bigger dividend doesn't mean a better company.

## Cash dividends

US companies usually pay dividends quarterly. Each dividend comes with several dates:

| Date | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Declaration date | The board announces the amount and dates |
| Ex-dividend date | Anyone buying on or after this day doesn't get this dividend |
| Record date | The company pays shareholders on its books as of this day |
| Payment date | The dividend arrives |

The key rule: **buy before the ex-dividend date to get the dividend.**

Since US stocks moved to T+1 settlement on May 28, 2024 (Lesson 10), the ex-dividend date and record date are now usually the same day.

### The price drops on the ex-dividend date

Say a stock is at $50 and pays a $0.50 dividend. On the ex-dividend date, the opening price is adjusted down by about $0.50, apart from the market's normal moves.

The reason: the $0.50 moves from the company's account to yours. You have $0.50 more cash and the shares are worth $0.50 less, so your total is unchanged. In a taxable account you also owe tax on the dividend (Lesson 21).

So buying just before the ex-dividend date to collect a dividend doesn't earn you extra money.

## Share buybacks

When a company buys back its shares in the market, it usually retires them, so fewer shares remain outstanding.

Say a company earns $100 million and has 100 million shares, so EPS is $1.00. After buying back and retiring 5% of its shares:

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Net income | $100 million | $100 million |
| Shares | 100 million | 95 million |
| EPS | $1.00 | About $1.05 |

You hold the same number of shares, but each share now represents a larger slice of the company.

Buybacks vs. dividends:

- **Tax**: dividends are taxed when you receive them; a buyback pays you nothing directly, so there's no taxable gain until you sell.
- **Flexibility**: markets usually treat a dividend cut as bad news; buybacks can be scaled up or down at any time.
- **Price**: a company that buys back stock when its price is very high is overpaying.

Since 2023, the US has charged a **1% excise tax** on stock buybacks by public companies, paid by the company.

::: warning Check what a buyback really does
Some companies buy back stock while issuing large amounts of new stock to employees as pay, so the total share count doesn't fall. To see whether buybacks actually reduce shares, track **shares outstanding** in the reports over several years.
:::

## Splits and reverse splits

A **stock split** turns each share into several, cutting the price in proportion. Your total value is unchanged.

NVIDIA, for example, split its stock 10-for-1 in June 2024: each share became 10, at about one tenth of the price. Hypothetical numbers below:

| | Before | After (10-for-1) |
|---|---|---|
| Shares you own | 10 | 100 |
| Share price | $1,000 | $100 |
| Total value | $10,000 | $10,000 |

Companies usually split when the price is high and they want shares to be easier to buy. Now that many brokers offer fractional shares (Lesson 10), that reason matters less.

A **reverse split** goes the other way, such as combining 10 shares into 1 at a higher price. A common reason is a price so low it risks breaking the exchange's listing requirements. Reverse splits often come from struggling companies and deserve caution.

Neither kind of split changes the value of what you own.

## Dividend reinvestment (DRIP)

Many brokers offer **dividend reinvestment**: dividends are automatically used to buy more of the same stock or fund, often in fractional shares.

- **Benefit**: no manual work, and dividends keep compounding (Lesson 1).
- **Tax**: in a taxable account, reinvested dividends are **still taxed in the year you receive them**.

Turning on dividend reinvestment is common for long-term index fund investors.

## Summary

- A company can reinvest profit, pay dividends or buy back stock; a bigger dividend doesn't mean a better company.
- **Buy before the ex-dividend date to get the dividend**; the price drops by about the dividend, so it isn't extra money.
- Buybacks reduce share count and lift EPS; check that the share count actually falls.
- Splits and reverse splits don't change what you own; reverse splits are more common at struggling companies.
- Dividend reinvestment is convenient, but dividends in a taxable account are still taxed.
