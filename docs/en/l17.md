## Option price = intrinsic value + time value

**Intrinsic value** is what the option would be worth if it expired right now.

- Call: stock price − strike (zero if negative)
- Put: strike − stock price (zero if negative)

**Time value** is the rest of the price. With time left, the stock can still move, and that possibility is worth something.

Example: the stock is at $52 and a $50-strike call is quoted at $3.50.

- Intrinsic value = 52 − 50 = $2
- Time value = 3.50 − 2 = $1.50

An out-of-the-money option has no intrinsic value; its whole price is time value. At expiration, time value goes to zero and only intrinsic value is left.

## What moves an option's price

| Change | Call | Put |
|---|---|---|
| Stock rises | Up | Down |
| Higher strike | Cheaper | More expensive |
| More time to expiration | Usually more expensive | Usually more expensive |
| Volatility rises | Up | Up |
| Interest rates rise | Slightly up | Slightly down |

The hardest of these to observe directly is **volatility**.

## Implied volatility

The more a stock swings, the more likely it makes a big move before expiration, and the more an option is worth.

**Implied volatility (IV)** is the volatility backed out of an option's market price. It reflects the market's expectation of future swings. Broker option chains usually show each option's IV.

- High IV: options are expensive. Common before earnings or big news, or in a panic.
- Low IV: options are cheap.

### The post-earnings IV drop

Before earnings, nobody knows the results, so IV is often high. Once they're out, uncertainty disappears and IV usually falls quickly.

An example estimated with the Black-Scholes model: the stock is at $100 and you buy a $100-strike call expiring in 14 days.

| | Before earnings | Day after earnings |
|---|---|---|
| Stock price | $100 | $102 (+2%) |
| Implied volatility | 60% | 30% |
| Option price | About $4.69 | About $3.42 |

You called the direction right and still lost about 27%. An option buyer needs the right direction and a big enough move to beat the IV drop and time decay.

## The Greeks

Brokers show several measures named after Greek letters. Each shows how sensitive the option's price is to one factor.

Take one call: stock $100, strike $100, 30 days to expiration, 30% implied volatility, priced at about $3.43 (Black-Scholes estimate).

| Greek | Meaning | This example | How to read it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Delta | Price change for a $1 stock move | About 0.52 | Stock +$1, option about +$0.52, about $52 per contract |
| Gamma | Delta change for a $1 stock move | About 0.05 | Stock +$1, delta goes from 0.52 to about 0.57 |
| Theta | Price change per day | About −0.06 | All else equal, about $0.06 a day lost, about $6 per contract |
| Vega | Price change for a 1-point IV move | About 0.11 | IV from 30% to 31%, option about +$0.11 |

Some patterns:

- **Delta**: calls range from 0 to 1, puts from −1 to 0. At-the-money options are about ±0.5, deep in the money near ±1, deep out of the money near 0.
- **Gamma**: largest at the money near expiration, when delta changes fast and prices swing hard.
- **Theta**: usually negative for buyers and positive for sellers. Sellers earn the passing of time value.
- **Vega**: larger with more time to expiration, so longer options are more sensitive to IV.

## Time decay

Time value doesn't shrink evenly; it shrinks faster as expiration nears.

Same option as above, assuming the stock stays at $100 and IV stays at 30% (Black-Scholes estimates):

| Days to expiration | Option price |
|---|---|
| 90 | About $5.94 |
| 60 | About $4.85 |
| 30 | About $3.43 |
| 14 | About $2.34 |
| 7 | About $1.66 |
| 1 | About $0.63 |

From 90 to 60 days the price drops about $1.09; in the last 30 days, the remaining $3.43 disappears entirely.

So short-dated at-the-money or out-of-the-money options look cheap, but they lose time value the fastest.

::: warning Models are estimates
Black-Scholes and similar models assume constant volatility and smooth price moves, which real markets don't follow. The numbers above illustrate patterns; actual prices are set by buyers and sellers.
:::

## Summary

- Option price = **intrinsic value** + **time value**; time value goes to zero at expiration.
- Higher **implied volatility** means pricier options; the post-earnings IV drop can cause losses even when you get the direction right.
- **Delta** is direction, **gamma** is how fast delta changes, **theta** is time decay, **vega** is volatility.
- Time value disappears faster as expiration nears.
