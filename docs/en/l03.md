## Foundation first, then the house

Many beginners want to open an account and buy stocks the moment they start learning. Experienced people ask a few questions first:

- If you lost your job next month, how would you pay the bills?
- Are you carrying high-interest debt?
- When do you plan to use this money?

Investing before answering these is like building on sand. One surprise, such as an illness, a layoff or a broken-down car, can force you to sell at the bottom. **This lesson is about the foundation you lay before investing.**

## The order of operations

Here's a widely used order. Everyone's situation differs, so adapt it, but the direction is worth following:

1. **Know where your money goes**: track spending for a month or two so you know your income and essential costs.
2. **Build a starter emergency fund**: about $1,000 or one month of expenses, for small surprises.
3. **Capture your employer's 401(k) match**: if your employer matches (next lesson), contribute at least enough to get the full match. It's free money.
4. **Pay off high-interest debt**, especially credit cards.
5. **Grow the emergency fund to 3–6 months of expenses.**
6. **Start long-term investing**: IRA, 401(k), taxable brokerage, by goal.

## Emergency fund: how much and where

### How much

A common guideline is **3 to 6 months of essential expenses**: rent, food, insurance, transportation and minimum debt payments. Not your whole income.

| Your situation | Lean toward |
|---|---|
| Stable income, single, no dependents | About 3 months |
| Irregular income (freelance, commission) | 6 months or more |
| Single-income household with kids | 6 months or more |
| Immigration status tied to your job | More is better |

### Where

An emergency fund needs to be **available any time and stable in value**. The return is secondary.

- **High-yield savings account**: many online banks pay noticeably more than traditional banks, and deposits are **FDIC-insured** (up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category).
- **Money market fund**: bought inside a brokerage account, very stable, usually yielding close to short-term rates. Note: it's **not FDIC-insured**, though historically problems have been very rare.
- **Short-term Treasury bills**: backed by the US government, with maturities from a few weeks to a year.

::: danger Not here
Stocks, crypto and options are **not** emergency funds. They may crash exactly when you need the money.
:::

## Pay off high-interest debt first

Say you owe on a credit card at 24% a year (many US cards charge over 20%) and you have $5,000 of spare cash. Invest it or pay the card?

- **Pay the card**: you save about $1,200 a year in interest. That's a **guaranteed**, **risk-free** 24% return.
- **Invest**: you may earn something over the long run, but no one can promise 24% a year, and you could lose money.

The answer is clear: **pay off high-interest debt first.** A simple rule of thumb:

| Debt rate | General approach |
|---|---|
| Above 8–10% (credit cards, some personal loans) | Pay it off first |
| Between 4% and 8% | Pay down and invest at the same time |
| Below 4% (some mortgages, student loans) | No rush to prepay; invest normally |

It's not a hard rule, but it helps you think clearly.

## Give every dollar a goal

Before investing, split your money by **when you'll need it**:

| Goal | When | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Emergency fund | Any time | High-yield savings, money market fund |
| Trip next year, new laptop | Within 1 year | Savings account, short-term Treasuries |
| Home down payment in 3 years | 2–5 years | Mostly stable assets |
| Retirement | 20+ years | Mostly stock index funds |

This does two things:

1. Each pot sits where it belongs, so short-term money isn't exposed to market swings.
2. When the market crashes, you know only your long-term money is down, which makes it much easier to stay calm.

## Example: Lin's first year

Lin just started working. Take-home pay is $4,500 a month, essential costs are $2,800, there's $3,000 of credit card debt, and the employer offers a 401(k) match.

1. Put $1,000 in a high-yield savings account as a starter emergency fund.
2. Set up the 401(k) contribution to get the full employer match.
3. Over the next few months, use the leftover money to clear the $3,000 card balance.
4. Then grow the emergency fund to about $10,000 (3–4 months of expenses).
5. After that, start monthly investing in index funds (Lessons 5–8).

Feels slow? Maybe. But by the time Lin really starts investing, no single surprise can force a sale.

## Summary

- Lay the foundation first: know your cash flow, build an emergency fund, clear high-interest debt.
- An emergency fund is usually **3–6 months of essential expenses**, kept safe and accessible.
- Paying off high-interest debt is a guaranteed high return.
- Give each pot of money a **goal and a deadline**; the deadline decides where it goes.
