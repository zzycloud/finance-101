::: danger Read this first
Options are far more complex and risky than stocks and funds. Bought options can lose everything within days; sold options can lose far more than the money received. This chapter is here so you can understand options, not to suggest trading them. If you only invest in index funds, you can ignore options entirely.
:::

## What an option is

An **option** is a contract. The buyer pays money for a **right**: to buy or sell a stock at a set price before a set date.

- **Call**: the right to **buy** the stock at the set price.
- **Put**: the right to **sell** the stock at the set price.

The buyer has a right, not an obligation: use it if it pays, let it go if not. The seller takes the money and the obligation: if the buyer exercises, the seller must sell (call) or buy (put) at the set price.

## Four basic terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Underlying | The stock or ETF the option is on |
| Strike price | The agreed buy or sell price |
| Expiration | The last day of the right |
| Premium | The price the buyer pays the seller |

For US stock options, **one contract usually covers 100 shares**. Prices are quoted per share: an option quoted at $2 costs $200 per contract.

## Calls: betting on a rise

Back to Maple Coffee, the hypothetical company from Lesson 11, now at $50. You buy one call: $55 strike, expiring in 3 months, $2 premium, so $200 per contract.

At expiration:

| Stock price | Option value | Profit/loss | Return |
|---|---|---|---|
| $50 | $0 | −$200 | −100% |
| $55 | $0 | −$200 | −100% |
| $57 | $200 | $0 | 0% |
| $60 | $500 | +$300 | +150% |
| $70 | $1,500 | +$1,300 | +650% |

- At $55 or below, the option expires worthless and the $200 is gone.
- The **breakeven** is the strike plus the premium: 55 + 2 = $57.
- The higher the stock goes, the more you make, with no ceiling.

Compare buying 100 shares for $5,000: at $60 you make $1,000 (+20%); if the stock doesn't move, you lose nothing. The option magnifies the percentage return with less money, but if the stock doesn't rise enough or fast enough, you lose the whole premium.

## Puts: betting on a fall

With the stock at $50, you buy one put: $45 strike, $1.50 premium, so $150 per contract.

| Stock price | Option value | Profit/loss |
|---|---|---|
| $50 | $0 | −$150 |
| $43.50 | $150 | $0 |
| $40 | $500 | +$350 |
| $30 | $1,500 | +$1,350 |

The further the stock falls, the more the put is worth, up to the stock reaching 0. Shareholders also use puts as insurance, covered in Lesson 18.

## In, at and out of the money

| | Call | Put |
|---|---|---|
| In the money | Stock above strike | Stock below strike |
| At the money | Stock about equal to strike | Stock about equal to strike |
| Out of the money | Stock below strike | Stock above strike |

At expiration, out-of-the-money options expire worthless.

## Buyers vs. sellers

| | Most you can make | Most you can lose |
|---|---|---|
| Buy a call | Unlimited | The premium |
| Sell a call (without owning the stock) | The premium | **Unlimited** |
| Buy a put | Strike − premium (stock goes to 0) | The premium |
| Sell a put | The premium | Strike − premium (stock goes to 0) |

The seller's income is capped while losses can be large. Selling a call without owning the stock is a **naked call**: however high the stock goes, you bear the loss, with no limit in theory.

## Exercise and closing out

US stock options are usually **American style**: they can be exercised on any trading day before expiration. So a seller can be required to perform at any time; this is called **assignment**.

Most option buyers never exercise. They **sell the option to close** before expiration, taking the gain or loss on the price.

## Getting approved to trade options

Brokerage accounts can't trade options by default; you have to apply. According to the SEC's investor site, investor.gov:

- The broker asks about your objectives, trading experience, income and net worth, and approves a **trading level** based on your answers. Brokers generally have 5 levels; higher levels allow riskier strategies.
- Before approval, the broker must give you the Options Clearing Corporation (OCC) booklet *Characteristics and Risks of Standardized Options*.

## Summary

- An option is a right: a **call** to buy, a **put** to sell. The buyer has the right; the seller has the obligation.
- One contract usually covers **100 shares**; prices are quoted per share.
- Buyers can lose at most the premium, but often lose all of it; sellers earn a capped amount and can lose a lot.
- Trading options requires separate broker approval and reading the OCC risk booklet.
