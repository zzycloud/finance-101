## Start with a question

Say you deposit $10,000 in a bank this year and leave it alone for ten years. When you take it out, it's all still there, plus a little interest. Did you make money or lose money?

In purchasing power, **you probably lost**, because of inflation.

This lesson covers two forces: **inflation**, which reduces what your money can buy, and **compounding**, which grows it.

## Inflation

**Inflation** means prices rise across the economy. A cup of coffee that cost $2 ten years ago might cost $3 today. When prices rise, the same dollars buy less. That's called a **loss of purchasing power**.

In the US, inflation is usually measured by the **CPI (Consumer Price Index)**, published monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). The Federal Reserve's long-run inflation target is **2%**, but actual inflation can run much higher: over the 12 months ended June 2022, the CPI rose 9.1%, the largest 12-month increase since 1981 (source: BLS).

Let's do the math:

| | Today | In 10 years |
|---|---|---|
| Account balance (0.5% interest) | $10,000 | about $10,511 |
| Price level (3% inflation a year) | 1.00× | about 1.34× |
| **Real purchasing power** | **$10,000** | **about $7,800** |

The number in the account went up, but it buys about 22% less. **Money left in a low-interest account for years loses purchasing power to inflation.**

::: tip Remember this
To know whether your money is *really* growing, check whether it's beating inflation. Returns after inflation are called **real returns**; returns before inflation are **nominal returns**.
:::

## Compounding: growth on top of growth

With **simple interest**, only your original money earns interest. With **compound interest**, the interest earns interest too.

Say you invest $10,000 and earn 7% a year:

- Year 1: you earn $700 and have $10,700
- Year 2: you don't earn $700 again; you earn $10,700 × 7% ≈ $749
- Year 3: you earn about $801…

The extra each year is small, but it accumulates:

| Years | Simple ($700 a year) | Compound (7%) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | $17,000 | about $19,672 |
| 20 | $24,000 | about $38,697 |
| 30 | $31,000 | about $76,123 |

After 30 years, compounding produces more than twice as much. Most of the late growth comes from "interest on interest".

::: warning About that 7%
The 7% here is just an assumption for illustration. **It is not a promise.** Real markets vary a lot from year to year, and some years lose money. The next lesson is all about that.
:::

## The Rule of 72

Here's a handy mental shortcut called the **Rule of 72**:

> **Years to double ≈ 72 ÷ annual return (%)**

- 6% a year: doubles in about 12 years
- 8% a year: doubles in about 9 years
- 12% a year: doubles in about 6 years

It works for inflation too: at 3% inflation, money loses half its purchasing power in about 24 years.

## Start early, and the gap grows

Two examples (assuming a 7% annual return, monthly contributions):

- **Alex** starts at 25, invests $300 a month for 10 years, then stops at 35 and lets the money grow untouched until 65. Total put in: **$36,000**.
- **Blake** starts at 35 and invests $300 a month all the way to 65, for 30 years. Total put in: **$108,000**.

Who has more at 65?

| | Put in | Roughly at 65 |
|---|---|---|
| Alex (early start, 10 years of saving) | $36,000 | about $390,000 |
| Blake (late start, 30 years of saving) | $108,000 | about $351,000 |

Alex put in a third as much and still ends up ahead, because Alex's money had 10 more years to compound.

Starting later is fine. The point is: **learn the basics, start as early as you can, and keep contributing.**

## Summary

- **Inflation** slowly shrinks idle money; judge returns by whether they beat inflation.
- **Compounding** is growth on growth; the longer it runs, the bigger the effect.
- **Rule of 72**: 72 ÷ return ≈ years to double.
- Compounding depends on **time**, so start early.
