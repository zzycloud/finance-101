## Why write it down

Lesson 22 showed that people make their worst decisions in big rallies and crashes. A written plan is for that version of you: when emotions run high, you don't rethink, you follow it.

Advisors and institutions in the US use a document called an **Investment Policy Statement (IPS)**. A personal version fits on one page and covers four things:

1. Why you invest: goals, time horizon, risk tolerance.
2. How you invest: allocation, specific funds, how money goes in.
3. How you maintain it: rebalancing rules, review schedule.
4. When you change it: what will and won't change the plan.

## Step 1: goals, horizon and risk tolerance

### Goals and horizon

Every dollar should have a purpose and a date. Different purposes call for different investments:

| Goal | Amount | When needed | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Emergency fund | 6 months of expenses | Any time | High-yield savings, money market fund (Lesson 3) |
| Home down payment | $60,000 | In 3 years | Cash, T-Bills, short-term Treasuries (Lesson 15) |
| Retirement | As much as possible | In 30 years | Index funds in a 401(k) and IRA (Lessons 4 and 8) |

Money needed within a few years doesn't belong in stocks. As Lesson 2 showed, stocks can lose half their value in a year or two, and you may not be able to wait for the recovery.

### Risk tolerance

Risk tolerance has two sides:

- **Ability**: how stable your income is, whether you have an emergency fund, how long until you need the money.
- **Willingness**: whether losses keep you up at night and make you want to sell.

Try this: your portfolio is worth $100,000 and a year later it's $60,000. What do you do? If the answer is "sell and never touch it again," your stock allocation should be lower. Choosing by how you'd really react works better than choosing by how much you think you should handle.

## Step 2: allocation and contributions

Write down:

- **Target allocation**: how much in stocks and bonds, and within stocks, how much US and international (Lesson 8).
- **Which funds**: tickers and expense ratios (Lesson 7).
- **How money goes in**: how much each month, on what day, into which account first.
- **What you won't do**: for example, no single stock above 5% of the total, no margin, no options or futures trading.

## Step 3: rebalancing and reviews

- **Review schedule**: once or twice a year, on fixed dates. You don't need to check your account daily.
- **Rebalancing rule**: adjust when any asset class is more than 5 percentage points off target, using new money first, then trades inside retirement accounts (Lesson 8).

## Step 4: when to change the plan

**Reasons to change it**:

- Life changes: marriage, children, a new job, job loss, an inheritance.
- Goal changes: the date you need the money gets close, or the goal itself changes.
- You find you misjudged your risk tolerance, for example a real crash proves more than you can bear.

**Not reasons to change it**:

- Big market rallies or crashes.
- News, expert forecasts, tips from friends.
- An asset that has done especially well or badly lately.

Give yourself a rule for changes too: write down the reason and wait a week. If you still think the change is right a week later, make it.

## Example

A hypothetical example, not a recommended allocation:

> **My Investment Plan (written November 2026)**
>
> I'm 30, working in the US, with a stable income and no high-interest debt.
>
> **Goals**: emergency fund of 6 months' expenses ($18,000) in a high-yield savings account; $60,000 home down payment in 3 years, held in T-Bills; for retirement, contribute enough to my 401(k) to get the full employer match, then fund a Roth IRA.
>
> **Allocation** (retirement money): 80% stocks (55% US, 25% international) + 20% bonds, using three low-cost index funds.
>
> **Contributions**: automatic after each paycheck: 401(k) up to the match, then Roth IRA, then a taxable brokerage account.
>
> **Rules**: review every January; rebalance with new money when more than 5 points off target; no single stock above 5%; no margin, no options or futures trading.
>
> **When to change**: reassess after marriage, children, a job change, or when the home purchase is less than a year away. A market drop doesn't change the plan; keep investing.

## The end of the course

Starting from compounding and inflation, you've covered funds, stocks, bonds, options, futures, taxes and behavior. For most people, what actually needs doing is short:

1. Build an emergency fund and pay off high-interest debt.
2. Use low-cost index funds, splitting stocks and bonds to fit your risk tolerance.
3. Invest automatically and rebalance on a schedule.
4. Write a plan and follow it when markets swing.

Everything else helps you understand the news, spot risks and avoid being carried off by complex products and scams.

## Summary

- An investment plan is written for the version of you in a big rally or crash.
- One page covers **goals and horizon, allocation, rebalancing rules and when to change it**.
- Money needed within a few years stays out of stocks; set risk tolerance by how you'd really react.
- Change the plan when your life changes, not when markets move.
