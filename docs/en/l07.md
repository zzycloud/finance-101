## Read the contents, not the name

There are thousands of ETFs listed in the US, and many have similar names: "US large cap", "total market", "tech", "high dividend", "2x", "inverse"…

A common beginner mistake is buying on the name or on recent returns. This lesson covers five things to check before buying an ETF.

Where's the information? Every issuer's website has a **fact sheet** and a **prospectus** for each ETF. Your broker's app usually shows the key details when you look up the **ticker**.

## Step 1: What does it track, and what's inside?

This is the most important step. Answer three questions:

1. **Which index does it track?** S&P 500, total market, one industry, one theme?
2. **What are the top 10 holdings and how much do they make up?** If the top 10 are more than half, it's concentrated.
3. **How is it split across industries and countries?** Is it piled into one sector?

| Type | Examples | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Broad-market ETFs | S&P 500, total market, global stocks | Diversified; good as a core holding |
| Sector ETFs | Tech, healthcare, energy | One industry; bigger swings |
| Thematic ETFs | AI, clean energy, EVs | Often launched when a theme is hottest; high risk of buying the top |
| Bond ETFs | Aggregate, Treasuries, corporates | Risk depends on maturity and credit quality |

::: tip Watch for overlap
VOO (S&P 500) and VTI (US total market) are different funds, but most of VTI's weight is the same large companies in the S&P 500. Owning both doesn't make you more diversified. Judge diversification by **what's inside**, not by how many funds you hold.
:::

## Step 2: Expense ratio

As the last lesson showed, cost is the thing you can reliably control.

- Many broad index ETFs charge **0.03%–0.10%**.
- Sector and thematic ETFs often cost more; 0.3%–0.8% is common.
- If two ETFs track the same index, the cheaper one is usually the better pick.

## Step 3: Size and trading volume

- **Size (AUM, assets under management)**: how much money the fund manages. Very small ETFs may be **closed** by the issuer if they aren't profitable. You get your money back, but you're forced to sell, which can trigger tax in a taxable account, and you have to reinvest.
- **Volume**: how much trades each day. More activity means smoother trading.

A simple rule for beginners: **prefer large, long-established broad-market ETFs.**

## Step 4: Bid-ask spread

At any moment on an exchange there are two prices:

- **Bid**: the most a buyer will pay right now, which is what you get when you sell.
- **Ask**: the least a seller will accept, which is what you pay when you buy.

The difference is the **spread**, a hidden trading cost.

> Example: bid $49.95, ask $50.05, spread $0.10, about 0.2%.

Big broad-market ETFs usually have spreads of a cent or two; obscure small ETFs can have much wider ones.

::: info Limit orders are safer
A **market order** fills immediately at the best available price. A **limit order** fills only at your price or better. Using limit orders for ETFs avoids surprise prices when spreads are wide or markets are wild. Try to avoid the first and last few minutes of the trading day, when spreads tend to be wider. Lesson 10 covers order types in detail.
:::

## Step 5: Tracking

An index fund aims to copy its index, but real results always differ slightly, mostly because of fees and trading costs.

- **Tracking difference**: the gap between the fund's return and the index's return over a period.
- A good index ETF has a small tracking difference, roughly equal to its expense ratio.

Fact sheets usually list fund and index returns side by side, so it's easy to compare.

## Beginners, stay away: leveraged and inverse ETFs

Some ETFs carry **2x, 3x, Ultra** (leveraged) or **Short, Inverse, −1x** (inverse) in their names. For example, "twice the index's daily return" or "up 1% when the index falls 1%".

The SEC and FINRA have specifically warned investors about these: most **reset daily**, and holding them longer than a day can produce results very different from what you'd expect.

A simple example: the index rises 10% on day one and falls 10% on day two.

| | Start | Day 1 (+10% / +20%) | Day 2 (−10% / −20%) | Two-day result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Index | 100 | 110 | 99 | **−1%** |
| 2x leveraged ETF | 100 | 120 | 96 | **−4%** |

The index fell 1%, but the 2x ETF fell 4%, not simply "twice". In a choppy market, this decay keeps adding up. An SEC investor bulletin gives an example where an index rose 2% over four months while a 2x leveraged ETF tracking it **fell 6%**.

::: danger Bottom line
Leveraged and inverse ETFs are tools for short-term traders who watch the market daily. They're **not for buy-and-hold investing**. Beginners should skip them, along with single-stock leveraged ETFs and futures-based ETFs.
:::

## Hands-on: three S&P 500 ETFs

| | VOO | IVV | SPY |
|---|---|---|---|
| Index | S&P 500 | S&P 500 | S&P 500 |
| Holdings | Nearly identical | Nearly identical | Nearly identical |
| Size | Very large | Very large | Very large |
| Expense ratio | 0.03% | 0.03% | about 0.09% |

All three hold almost the same stocks. For a long-term holder, **the cheaper one is the better choice**. SPY's biggest advantage is its enormous trading volume, which matters to short-term traders and institutions, not much to long-term investors. (VOO and SPY expense ratios as reported in June 2026; IVV from the iShares website in November 2026. Check the issuers' websites for current figures.)

## Checklist

Before buying any ETF, ask yourself:

- ☐ Do I know which index it tracks and what its top holdings are?
- ☐ How much does it overlap with what I already own?
- ☐ What's the expense ratio? Is there a cheaper equivalent?
- ☐ Is it large and actively traded?
- ☐ Does the name include 2x, 3x, Ultra, Short or Inverse?
- ☐ Am I buying it because it fits my plan, or because it went up recently?

## Summary

- Look at **what's inside** an ETF before its name.
- Among similar products, favor **low cost, large size, active trading**.
- The spread is a hidden cost; **limit orders** are safer.
- **Leveraged and inverse ETFs** reset daily and aren't for long-term holding. Beginners, stay away.
