## Why beginners should start with funds

Lesson 2 showed that diversification reduces the damage from any one company. But buying 500 stocks yourself takes a lot of money and effort.

**Funds** solve that problem.

## How a fund works: pool money, buy together

Say 1,000 people each chip in $100, making $100,000, and hand it to a professional manager. The manager buys stocks in hundreds of companies. Each person owns a slice of that basket in proportion to what they put in.

A fund has three parties:

- **You**: own a big basket of assets with little money, automatically diversified.
- **The fund company**: handles buying, selling, recordkeeping and custody arrangements, for a small fee.
- **The custodian bank**: holds the fund's assets separately from the fund company, to prevent misuse.

```
You + other investors ──money──▶ Fund ──buys──▶ stocks / bonds of hundreds of companies
                     ◀──share in gains, losses and dividends──
```

## NAV: what one share is worth

**NAV (Net Asset Value)** is the value of one share of the fund:

> **NAV = (total assets − liabilities) ÷ shares outstanding**

If a fund holds $10 million of stock and has 500,000 shares, each share's NAV is $20.

When the stocks inside go up, the NAV goes up; when they fall, it falls. Your return comes from NAV growth plus any dividends the fund pays out.

## Three names that confuse everyone

Beginners often mix up **mutual fund**, **ETF** and **index fund**. They describe two different dimensions:

- **Mutual fund vs. ETF**: *how you buy and sell* it.
- **Active vs. index fund**: *how it picks investments*.

So an "index fund" can be either a mutual fund or an ETF.

### Mutual fund vs. ETF

| | Mutual fund | ETF (exchange-traded fund) |
|---|---|---|
| How you buy | Through the fund company or a broker | On an exchange, like a stock |
| Price you get | The next NAV calculated after your order, usually once per business day after the close | Live market price during trading hours |
| Minimum | Some require $1,000–$3,000 | One share; many brokers allow fractional shares |
| Tax efficiency in taxable accounts | Average | Usually better (the structure tends to distribute fewer capital gains) |
| Automatic monthly investing | Very easy to set a fixed dollar amount | Supported at some brokers |

**Example**: you want to buy at 10 a.m.

- An **ETF** fills at 10 a.m. at the market price.
- A **mutual fund** order waits until the NAV is calculated after the close and fills at that price.

For long-term investors the difference is small. **Use whichever is more convenient.**

::: info Can an ETF's price drift from its NAV?
An ETF's market price is set by buyers and sellers, so it can differ from the NAV in theory. But ETFs have a creation/redemption mechanism: large institutions can swap baskets of stock for ETF shares and back. If the price drifts too far, they arbitrage it back. For large, heavily traded ETFs, the price and NAV are usually very close.
:::

### Active vs. index funds

| | Actively managed fund | Index (passive) fund |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Beat the market | Match the market |
| How it picks | A manager researches and chooses | Buys whatever is in the index, by its rules |
| Cost | Usually higher | Usually very low |
| Long-run results | Most lag their index | Index return minus a small fee |

Why do most active funds lag? That's the next lesson.

## Expense ratio

The **expense ratio** is the annual fee a fund charges as a percentage of your assets.

- You don't get a bill. The fee is **deducted from the fund's assets a little each day** and reflected in the NAV.
- So you'll never see a "fee" line in your account, but it's always happening.

| Expense ratio | Yearly cost per $10,000 invested |
|---|---|
| 0.03% (many large index funds) | $3 |
| 0.20% | $20 |
| 0.50% | $50 |
| 1.00% (many active funds) | $100 |

A few dollars a year is small, but the cost compounds, and over decades the gap gets large. The next lesson's calculator shows it.

::: warning Other fees to watch
Some mutual funds charge **loads** (sales charges) when you buy or sell, sometimes several percent. Some brokers also charge a transaction fee for other companies' mutual funds. Read all the fees in the fund's documents before buying.
:::

## Funds still carry risk

A fund is just a container. Its risk depends on what's inside:

- Holding the whole stock market, it swings with the market.
- Holding short-term Treasuries, it's very stable.
- Holding one hot industry or one country, it can swing more than the broad market.

**Diversification reduces single-company risk, but not market-wide risk.** Funds aren't guaranteed and aren't FDIC-insured. SIPC only protects your assets if the broker fails; it doesn't cover the fund's ups and downs.

## Summary

- **Funds** let you own a basket of assets with little money, diversified automatically.
- **NAV** is the value of one share.
- **Mutual fund vs. ETF** is about how you trade; **active vs. index** is about how it picks.
- The **expense ratio** is deducted automatically; small-looking, but powerful over time.
